# Phase 491: Provenance Chain

**Phase:** 491 (ONGOING)
**Last updated:** 2026-02-28
**Status:** Complete ownership chain documented; two gaps identified; leading acquisition theory (Widemann-Rauwolf) detailed

## Purpose

Document the complete ownership history of the Voynich manuscript (Beinecke MS 408) from creation to present, identifying gaps, evaluating competing theories, and noting convergences with structural analysis.

---

## The Complete Chain

### Phase 1: Creation (1404-1438)

**Radiocarbon dating (University of Arizona, 2009):** AMS dating of four vellum samples from folios 8, 26, 47, and 68. The 95% confidence interval: **1404-1438 CE** for the death of the animals whose skin became the parchment.

- **Material:** Calfskin (protein testing, 2014)
- **Not a palimpsest:** Multispectral analysis confirms the vellum had not been written on before
- **Ink:** Iron gall, applied with quill pen, minor compositional variation across manuscript (McCrone Associates, 2009)
- **Multiple scribes:** At least five scribal hands (Lisa Fagin Davis); production was institutional, not individual
- **Forgery ruled out:** Radiocarbon consistency across pages; inconceivable that 14-15 entire unused calfskins from the early 15th century could have survived for later use

### Phase 2: The 160-Year Gap (c. 1440 - c. 1590s)

**No documented record** of the manuscript for approximately 160 years after its estimated creation. This is the largest lacuna in the provenance chain.

### Phase 3: Possible Rauwolf Ownership (speculative, pre-1596)

If the Widemann-Rauwolf theory is correct (see below), the manuscript may have been in **Leonhard Rauwolf's** personal library in Augsburg before his death in 1596. Rauwolf was a famous botanist who traveled extensively and collected manuscripts. However, this remains unproven.

### Phase 4: Karl Widemann (c. 1597-1599)

After Rauwolf's widow's death in 1597, Widemann likely acquired books from the Rauwolf estate. On **March 1, 1599**, he sold "a small barrel with a couple of remarkable/rare books" to Rudolf II for **500 Taler / 600 Rheinische Gulden** (see Widemann-Rauwolf theory below).

### Phase 5: Emperor Rudolf II (1599 - c. 1608)

Rudolf owned the manuscript during the final years of his reign. He moved his permanent residence from Vienna to Prague in 1583 and established a legendary Kunstkammer and personal library at Prague Castle (Hradschin). The Voynich does NOT appear in the Froeschl inventory of the Kunstkammer (1607-1611) — it was likely stored in Rudolf's personal library, a distinct collection.

Rudolf died in 1612, still owing money to Horcicky.

### Phase 6: Jacobus Horcicky de Tepenec (c. 1608 - 1622)

Rudolf likely gave the manuscript to Horcicky, possibly as partial payment for debts. Horcicky's erased name on folio 1r (confirmed under UV light) supports his ownership. He died **September 25, 1622**. His will left his possessions to the Jesuits, but the Voynich lacks the Jesuit library mark found on his other books — suggesting the manuscript left his hands before death or was separated from his estate before cataloguing.

### Phase 7: Georg Baresch (by 1637 - c. 1650-1662)

By 1637, the manuscript had been in Baresch's library for "many years." He described it as a "Sphinx" that occupied "useless space." Baresch was a Prague citizen, court reporter, and alchemist who had studied at the Clementinum (the same Jesuit institution where Horcicky was based) and at the Sapienza in Rome.

The Baresch-Kircher correspondence (1637-1639) contains the **earliest surviving mentions** of the manuscript. Baresch guessed it was "Egyptian science" and identified the content as medical, herbal, and chemical.

### Phase 8: Jan Marek Marci (c. 1650s - 1665)

Marci inherited the Voynich from Baresch. Chief Physician of Bohemia, Professor and Rector of Charles University. Sent the manuscript to Athanasius Kircher in Rome with a cover letter (August 19, 1665/1666) reporting the 600-ducat purchase by Rudolf II and the Roger Bacon attribution — information from **Raphael Mnishovsky** (d. 1644), who was himself a cryptographer and alchemist.

**2024 multispectral imaging** revealed Marci's handwriting on fol. 1r: three parallel columns attempting substitution decipherment (Roman alphabet, Voynichese, offset Roman alphabet). The cipher produced nonsense — the manuscript is not a simple substitution.

### Phase 9: Athanasius Kircher and the Collegio Romano (1666 - c. 1870)

Kircher received the manuscript in Rome. No evidence he ever responded to Marci about it or made progress deciphering it. The manuscript remained in the Collegio Romano library for approximately 200 years with no documented references.

### Phase 10: The Jesuit Concealment (1870-1912)

In 1870, Italian unification led to confiscation of Church properties. Petrus Beckx, Superior General of the Jesuits, classified manuscripts as his "private library" to exempt them. Approximately 60 cases of manuscripts were transferred to **Villa Torlonia in Castel Gandolfo** (NOT Villa Mondragone in Frascati, as long believed — corrected by Zandbergen 2023).

### Phase 11: Wilfrid Voynich (1912 - 1930)

In 1912, the Jesuits sold the main collection to the Vatican Library (approximately 380 manuscripts). A smaller set was sold to **Wilfrid Voynich**, a Polish-Lithuanian antiquarian book dealer, for approximately 500 Lire per volume. The intermediary was Father Joseph Strickland S.J. Voynich honored strict confidentiality agreements throughout his life.

### Phase 12-13: Ethel Voynich (1930-1960) and Anne Nill (1960-1961)

Inherited by Voynich's widow, then by his secretary Anne Nill.

### Phase 14: Hans Peter Kraus (1961-1969)

Nill sold to Kraus for $24,500. Kraus attempted to sell for $160,000 but found no buyer.

### Phase 15: Yale / Beinecke Library (1969 - present)

Kraus donated the manuscript to Yale. Catalogued as MS 408 in the Beinecke Rare Book and Manuscript Library.

## Key Person: Jacobus Horcicky de Tepenec

### Biography

**Born:** c. 1575, Borenovice, Moravia (lower-class family).

**Name etymology:** Czech "horcice" = mustard; Latinized "Sinapius" from "sinapis" = mustard (Latin). The name and the product are linked.

### Career

| Year | Event |
|------|-------|
| c. 1590 | Admitted to Jesuit seminary at Cesky Krumlov; trained in pharmacy under Martin Schaffner |
| 1598 | Studied Aristotelian philosophy at the Clementinum, Prague |
| 1602 | Studying logic at the Clementinum |
| c. 1600-1606 | Created **Aqua Sinapis** (distilled mustard water) — made him extremely wealthy |
| 1606 | Capitaneus of St. George's Convent properties at Prague Castle |
| 1607 | Named **Imperial Chemist** (imperial distiller/pharmacist) by Rudolf II; also curator of Rudolf's botanical gardens and personal physician |
| Winter 1608 | Cured Rudolf II of grave illness that other doctors had failed to remedy |
| Oct 20, 1608 | **Ennobled** by Rudolf with title "de Tepenec" |
| 1616 | Leader of the Melnik township |
| 1620 | Jailed during Bohemian Revolt; exchanged and exiled; returned to Melnik |
| Sept 25, 1622 | Died at the Clementinum from complications of a horse-fall injury |

### Aqua Sinapis

Horcicky's fortune came from distilling a preparation called **Aqua Sinapis** — a distilled mustard water, likely medicinal. He established an herb laboratory at the **Smichov botanical garden** in Prague. He lent Rudolf II enormous sums and received the estate of Melnik in return. Upon death he left **50,000 gold coins** and the Melnik estate to the Jesuits.

### The Missing Jesuit Library Mark

Five books bearing Horcicky's ex libris survive today:

| # | Work | Type | Date | Current Location | Jesuit Mark? |
|---|------|------|------|-----------------|--------------|
| 1 | Boethius, *Dialectica Aristotelis* | Print | 1553 | Czech National Library | YES |
| 2 | Bartholomeus Anglicus, *De Proprietatu Rerum* | MS | 1480 | Strahov Monastery | YES |
| 3 | Petrus de Crescentiis, *Opus Ruralium Commodorum* | Print | 1486 | Strahov Monastery | YES |
| 4 | **The Voynich Manuscript** | MS | early 15th C | Beinecke Library | **NO** |
| 5 | Albertanus Brixiensis, *De doctrina dicendi et tacendi* (Czech) | MS | Unknown | Charles University | — |

Books 1-3 all bear a Jesuit library ownership note. The Voynich does NOT. Rene Zandbergen personally examined the entire first page under UV light and confirmed no such note exists. Horcicky's will was discovered without an accompanying book list, and related materials were destroyed.

**Implication:** Either the Voynich left Horcicky's hands before death, or it was separated from his estate before Jesuit cataloguing. This is a critical anomaly in the provenance chain.

## The Widemann-Rauwolf Acquisition Theory (Guzy 2022-2023)

### The Research

**Stefan Guzy** (University of the Arts Bremen) presented at the First International Conference on the Voynich Manuscript (University of Malta, November 2022). Paper: "Book Transactions of Emperor Rudolf II, 1576-1612: New Findings on the Earliest Ownership of the Voynich Manuscript."

### Karl Widemann (1555 - after 1629)

- Born August 2, 1555, Munich. Family moved to Augsburg (Protestant).
- Studied at Ingolstadt, Leipzig, **University of Padua** (doctorate), Dole (medical doctorate)
- 1584 onward: prolific manuscript collector in Augsburg
- **1587-1588: Worked in Prague as secretary to Edward Kelley** at Rudolf II's court
- After Prague: medical doctor in Augsburg; married into the Zimmermann family
- Dedicated 30+ years to copying and collecting Paracelsus manuscripts
- Compiled library catalogue (completed 1629, preserved in Kassel)

### Leonhard Rauwolf (1535-1596)

- Born June 21, 1535, Augsburg. Studied medicine at Montpellier.
- Created multi-volume herbaria: 600+ specimens from France/Italy (1560-1563), 900 specimens from Levant/Mesopotamia (1573-1575)
- First European botanist of post-medieval era to travel in Syria and Mesopotamia
- 1588: Forced to leave Augsburg (Protestant); moved to Linz, **retained Augsburg house**
- 1593: His herbals sold to Rudolf II via botanist Carolus Clusius
- 1596: Died at Waitzen (Hungary), serving with imperial troops
- 1597: His widow died; Augsburg house and belongings sold

### The Connection

Widemann **lived in Rauwolf's former house** in Augsburg. He was Rauwolf's successor as city physician. After Rauwolf's widow's death (1597), Widemann likely handled the sale of Rauwolf's remaining books.

### The 600-Ducat Match

**Guzy's methodology:** Compiled all book-related transactions from imperial account books (Hofkammer journals) in Vienna and Prague. Out of nearly **7,000 journal entries** including **126 book transactions**, only **one** matched 600 gold coins:

| Field | Value |
|-------|-------|
| **Date** | March 1, 1599 |
| **Seller** | Carl Widemann |
| **Amount** | 500 Taler (= 600 Rheinische Gulden) |
| **Description** | "a small barrel with a couple of remarkable/rare books" ("etlich rare bucher") |
| **Archival source** | Geizkofler family archives, Ludwigsburg (the Geizkoflers were imperial treasurers) |

The Marci letter (1665) reports that Rudolf paid **600 ducats** for the Voynich (via Mnishovsky). Ducats and Rheinische Gulden were near-equivalent gold denominations. The amount matches, and no other book transaction in Rudolf's records involves 600 gold coins.

**What Guzy did NOT prove:** The archival records describe "remarkable/rare books" — the Voynich is never named specifically. Nor can it be proven that the books originated from Rauwolf rather than Widemann's own collection. The theory is circumstantial but compelling.

### The Widemann-Padua-Prague Triangle

Widemann studied at the **University of Padua** — the same institution at the center of our Phase 491 historical network (Fontana, Galeazzo, the Rupescissa Glossator). He then worked in **Prague** as secretary to Edward Kelley. This places the probable seller of the Voynich at two of the three key nodes in our network (Padua and Prague), though his time at Padua (late 1570s-early 1580s) was a century after the Voynich's creation.

## Prague Court Context

### Rudolf II and the Sciences

Rudolf moved from Vienna to Prague in 1583, establishing Prague Castle as the center of the Empire. His court attracted astronomers (Tycho Brahe, Kepler), alchemists (Kelley, Sendivogius), and artists (Arcimboldo). His Kunstkammer was one of the greatest in European history.

### The Kunstkammer Inventory

The Froeschl inventory (1607-1611) catalogued the Kunstkammer. The **Voynich does NOT appear** in it. Neither do the Widemann books nor the Sambucus classical manuscripts. These were stored in Rudolf's **personal library** at the Hradschin, distinct from the Kunstkammer.

### The Swedish Sack (1648)

Swedish troops looted Prague Castle, taking hundreds of paintings, bronzes, instruments, and curiosities. The Voynich **survived** because it was in private hands (Baresch) by this time, not in institutional collections.

### Hugo Blotius and Vienna-Prague Transfers

Blotius (1533-1608), first Imperial Librarian in Vienna, facilitated transfers of alchemical and magical texts from the Viennese court library to Rudolf's personal collections in Prague. This documents an active institutional channel through which manuscripts — including potentially old Vienna medical faculty manuscripts — could have reached Prague.

### Key Court Figures

| Person | Dates | Role | Voynich Connection |
|--------|-------|------|-------------------|
| Thaddaeus Hagecius | 1525-1600 | Court polymath, astronomer | Hosted Dee and Kelley; intermediary for acquisitions |
| John Dee | 1527-1608/9 | English mathematician/occultist | Visited Prague 1584-1589; earlier Voynich theories (now superseded) |
| Edward Kelley | 1555-1597/8 | English alchemist | **Widemann worked as Kelley's secretary 1587-1588** |
| Michael Sendivogius | 1566-1636 | Polish alchemist | Courtier at Rudolf's court; supported by Mnishovsky |
| Raphael Mnishovsky | 1580-1644 | Royal secretary, cryptographer | Source of the 600-ducat and Roger Bacon claims |

## 2024 Multispectral Imaging

In 2014, the Lazarus Project team (Phelps, Heyworth, Van Duzer, Boydston, Easton) captured multispectral images of 10 selected folios. Due to staff turnover, platform development, and COVID, results were not published until **September 2024**, when Roger Easton (RIT) reprocessed the raw 16-bit TIFFs.

### Key Findings

**Fol. 1r — Marci's decipherment attempt:** Three parallel columns of faded lettering in the right margin:
- Column 1: Roman alphabet (a-z)
- Column 2: Voynichese characters
- Column 3: Roman alphabet offset by one letter

This is an early substitution cipher attempt. It produces nonsense — confirming the manuscript is not a simple substitution cipher. Paleographic comparison attributed the annotations to **Jan Marek Marci** (matching letterforms from c. 1640).

**Fol. 1r — Tepenec signature:** UV imaging confirmed "Jacobi a Tepenecz" in the lower margin.

**Fols. 17r and 116v:** Mysterious marginal inscriptions with "Germanic features," roughly contemporary with the manuscript, possibly incantations or charms. Previously illegible.

**Fol. 93r:** Pigment stain from a flower confirmed that **images were drawn and colored before the text was written** — establishing production sequence.

**Fol. 26r:** Offset evidence from facing page, potentially useful for reconstructing original leaf sequence.

**Fol. 102v1:** Post-processing revealed **drawings beneath stains** and previously invisible text on vessel areas.

## The Convergence

The provenance chain produces a striking convergence with structural analysis:

The **first confirmed owner** whose name physically appears on the manuscript — **Jacobus Horcicky de Tepenec** — was, by profession, an **imperial distiller**. His fortune came from **Aqua Sinapis** (distilled mustard water). He ran herb laboratories and botanical gardens. He was appointed **Imperial Chemist**.

This project's independent structural analysis of the Voynich's Currier B text concludes it encodes "a family of closed-loop, kernel-centric control programs designed to maintain a system within a narrow viability regime, governed by a single shared grammar" — a description consistent with distillation or process-control procedures.

These two evidence streams are entirely independent:
1. **Structural analysis** derived from statistical properties of token sequences (1,221 validated constraints)
2. **Provenance** derived from physical evidence (UV signature) and archival records

The user observed: "the fact that the first recorded owner of the manuscript was a distiller is super strong. particularly considering we derived the distillation narrative and didn't start with it."

Two interpretive scenarios:
1. **In situ use:** The manuscript was actively used in Horcicky's distillation operations — it was an operational manual for the laboratory he ran
2. **Lab succession:** The manuscript was acquired along with laboratory equipment, personnel, or materials when Horcicky set up or expanded his operations — a common mode of knowledge transfer in the period

Both scenarios are consistent with a manuscript encoding distillation procedures reaching the hands of a professional distiller. Neither proves the connection — this convergence is suggestive, not conclusive.

## Provenance Gaps and Open Questions

### Gap 1: The 160-Year Lacuna (c. 1440 - c. 1590s)

The largest gap. If the manuscript was produced c. 1404-1438 in the Vienna-Padua zone (as our historical network suggests), how did it reach Augsburg or Prague by the late 1590s?

Possible channels:
- **Monastery network:** Melk, Tegernsee, and Klosterneuburg monasteries connected Vienna to Bavaria and beyond
- **University dispersal:** Vienna Medical Faculty manuscripts could have been sold, inherited, or dispersed after faculty members' deaths
- **Puff's unpublished experimenta:** If the Voynich was among Puff's materials, it could have entered private circulation after his death (1473)
- **Blotius transfers:** Alchemical manuscripts moved from Vienna to Prague at Rudolf's orders in the 1580s-1590s
- **Book trade:** The 15th-century book trade connected Italian, German, and Bohemian cities

### Gap 2: Horcicky to Baresch (1622-1637)

Horcicky died in 1622. By 1637, Baresch had possessed the manuscript for "many years." Both men were connected to the Clementinum. The missing Jesuit library mark suggests the manuscript did not pass through the Jesuit library, but the institutional overlap (same building, same decade) suggests a more direct transfer.

### Gap 3: Baresch Acquisition

How did Baresch acquire the manuscript? He was an alchemist and antique collector — but where did he find it? The Clementinum connection to Horcicky is suggestive but unproven.
