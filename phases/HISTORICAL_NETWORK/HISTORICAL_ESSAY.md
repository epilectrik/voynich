## 1. The Window

The Voynich manuscript's vellum dates to 1404–1438 by radiocarbon analysis. The manuscript has at least five scribal hands (Lisa Fagin Davis), meaning its production was institutional — not one person working alone, but a coordinated effort by a group with shared training, materials, and purpose.

This essay maps the institutional, intellectual, and material infrastructure that existed in the Voynich's temporal-geographic zone. It does not identify the author or decode the text. It establishes what was possible, what was practiced, and what remains to be found.

---

## 2. The Pipeline: Padua to Vienna

The most important institutional fact about Central European medicine in the fifteenth century is the pipeline between the University of Padua and the University of Vienna. Students from the German-speaking world traveled south to Padua for medical training, then returned north carrying knowledge, manuscripts, and professional networks.

This was not occasional traffic. Enea Silvio Piccolomini (later Pope Pius II), writing from Vienna in 1444, observed that "many will remain in Vienna who now go to Padua" — acknowledging both the traffic and Vienna's ambition to rival it.

### The Key Transfer: Galeazzo di Santa Sofia

The pipeline's most consequential moment for our purposes occurred in 1404. **Galeazzo di Santa Sofia**, a member of Padua's most prominent medical dynasty, had been teaching at the University of Vienna since 1394/95. He served as personal physician to Duke Albrecht IV of Austria. In 1404 — the first year of the Voynich's radiocarbon window — he performed the first public anatomical dissection north of the Alps, in Vienna.

In 1406 Galeazzo returned to Padua, where he taught theoretical medicine until his death from plague in 1427. During this second Padua period, he served as one of the degree promoters for **Giovanni Fontana**, a physician-engineer who would become the only documented Padua scholar to create an entirely invented cipher alphabet.

This makes Galeazzo a documented human bridge: he spent a decade in Vienna during the exact years the Voynich vellum was produced, then returned to Padua and personally mentored a cipher-maker. The connection is not speculative — it is recorded in university degree records.

### Other Pipeline Traffic

Galeazzo was not the only connection:

- **Giovanni de Dondi** (d.1389), the famous Padua physician-astronomer, left manuscripts that ended up in the Austrian National Library (ÖNB Lat. 5208).
- **Georg Mustinger**, prior of the Augustinian house at Klosterneuburg (d.1442), spent **500 florins in 1421 purchasing books from Padua** — an enormous sum that documents institutional-level knowledge acquisition.
- **Georg Peuerbach** (1423–1461) and his student **Regiomontanus** (1436–1476), Vienna's great astronomers, both traveled to Italy. Regiomontanus lectured at Padua in 1464.
- **Johannes de Ketham**, a contemporary colleague of Michael Puff at Vienna, published his *Fasciculus medicinae* in Venice in 1491 — the pipeline running in reverse, with Vienna medical knowledge reaching Italian print.

## 3. The Distillation Chain

The Voynich's structural grammar, as analyzed in our project's 1,220 validated constraints, encodes closed-loop control programs consistent with industrial process control — specifically, the kind of monitoring, thermal management, and material-flow operations involved in distillation. The historical chain of distillation knowledge is therefore directly relevant.

### From Bologna to Strasbourg (1280–1512)

The chain begins with **Taddeo Alderotti** (Bologna, c.1280), who developed fractional distillation — separating compounds by vaporization temperature using multiple successive passes. His Latin *consilia* on aqua vitae circulated in manuscript, and a German vernacular adaptation (the *Branntweintraktat*) survives in seven known copies, including **Klosterneuburg Cod. 681** (1396) — a medical compendium produced eight years before the Voynich vellum dating begins, at an Augustinian house 13 km from Vienna.

The Klosterneuburg copy is bilingual: German prose on the medical virtues of distilled wine (fols. 8v–9r), followed by Latin recipes with explicit multi-stage distillation procedures — "in prima distillatione," "in secunda distillatione" — including a 10:1 distillation yield ratio and the instruction "leni igne" (gentle fire), documenting temperature awareness.

From Alderotti, the chain runs through:

- **Arnald of Villanova** (Montpellier, c.1300) — expanded distilled alcohol into systematic medical use
- **John of Rupescissa** (Avignon, 1351) — declared the quintessence (fifth essence, extracted by distillation) a universal medicine in *De consideratione quintae essentiae*, which circulated in at least 10 German manuscripts by the fifteenth century
- **An unidentified glossator** (Padua, 1463) — publicly defended quintessence theory "in einer offenbarlichen versamlung der artzet" (in a public assembly of physicians)
- **Michael Puff von Schrick** (Vienna, c.1455) — produced the *Büchlein von den ausgebrannten Wässern*, a distillation manual organized by plant material (84 chapters)
- **Hieronymus Brunschwig** (Strasbourg, 1500/1512) — published the knowledge in print, ending the manuscript-only era

### The Glossator: A Ghost at the Center

Brunschwig's 1512 *Large Book of Distillation* does not cite Rupescissa directly. His primary quintessence authority is "der glosierer uber Johannes Rupescissus" — a commentator who publicly defended quintessence theory at Padua in 1463. This person is unidentified. Their public defense occurred one year before Regiomontanus lectured at Padua (1464), placing them at the intersection of the Vienna–Padua pipeline during the peak of fifteenth-century knowledge transfer.

Brunschwig also does not cite Michael Puff by name — in either his 1500 *Small Book* or his 1512 *Large Book*. Modern scholars have identified word-for-word incorporation of Puff's material into the Small Book through textual comparison. The transmission chain is real but invisible at the citation level: Puff's procedural knowledge was absorbed into the published tradition without acknowledgment.

## 4. The Secrecy Regime

The Voynich manuscript is written entirely in an undeciphered script. If it encodes procedural knowledge (as our structural analysis suggests), the question is: why encode it? The answer lies in documented institutional secrecy practices at exactly the institutions connected to the Voynich's probable milieu.

### Sworn Oaths at Vienna

The Vienna Medical Faculty *Acta* (1436–1501) document that new deans swore an oath: "*singula facultatis secreta nullatenus revelare velit*" — to reveal none of the faculty's secrets. This is not metaphorical. The faculty maintained operational secrecy as institutional policy.

**Michael Puff von Schrick** served as dean eleven times between 1435 and 1473. During this period, he personally held the *registrum receptarum* — the faculty's pharmaceutical recipe register — under his personal custody. This document, if it survives, would show what notation the Vienna Medical Faculty used for procedural knowledge during the exact period of the Voynich.

A clandestine dissection in 1452 was handled "*occulte*" — secretly. And a later testimony by Stainpeis (c.1520) records that Puff left "*multa egregia experimenta*" (many excellent experiments/recipes) whose fame "has not been extinguished" — implying unpublished operational knowledge surviving in manuscript form after Puff's death.

### The Secretum Secretorum: Cultural Norm

The *Secretum Secretorum* — a pseudo-Aristotelian text framed as private instruction from Aristotle to Alexander — circulated in over 600 Latin copies and established the cultural equation that important knowledge equals secrets. A German version (G3) appears in Klosterneuburg Cod. 681 (1396), interwoven with Ortolf von Baierland's *Arzneibuch* in a unified medical compilation. Its secrecy doctrine is explicit: knowledge is "a secret that human hearts can scarcely bear," accessible only to the morally and intellectually qualified.

This was not eccentric — it was the mainstream position. Brunschwig's 1512 revelation that he was breaking the secrecy norm by publishing distillation knowledge was itself a major cultural statement. He named Arnoldus de Villa Nova and Marsilio Ficino as prior custodians of the *Aurum potabile* secret "which one should keep in great safeguarding and concealment" — then broke that tradition, saying "*het mich nit bezwungen die lieb*" — "had not love compelled me."

The word "geoffenbart" (revealed/disclosed) appears **90 times** in Brunschwig's 1512 text. It is not a casual word — it is the operating thesis of the entire book. The frequency of its use documents that Brunschwig understood himself as performing a transgressive act: bringing hidden knowledge into the light.

### Regiomontanus: Secrecy in Practice

The astronomer Regiomontanus, who lectured at Padua in 1464, was operating in Nuremberg by 1473 under extreme secrecy. The Schedel correspondence from that year documents a **locked house** with secret printing operations and private trips to Italy specifically to acquire books. His associate Hartmann Schedel — a physician trained at Padua — never once mentions alchemy or distillation in surviving correspondence, a silence that is either remarkable disinterest or deliberate omission.

## 5. The Cipher Parallels

Between 1379 and 1499, cipher use in the Voynich's geographic zone is documented in at least four domains: diplomatic correspondence, alchemical manuscripts, technical treatises, and theoretical cryptography. The Voynich is not an anomaly — it fits into a well-documented tradition of encoded knowledge.

### Fontana: Invented Glyphs at Padua (c.1420)

**Giovanni Fontana** (medical degree, Padua, 1421) used an entirely invented alphabet — 23 symbols constructed exclusively from circles and straight lines — in two manuscripts: the *Bellicorum instrumentorum liber* (c.1420, BSB Cod.icon. 242) and the *Secretum de thesauro experimentorum* (c.1430, BnF). The system was fully deciphered by Battisti and Saccaro in 1984: simple monoalphabetic substitution of Latin.

Fontana's design is systematic:
- **Vowels** share a circle base, distinguished by directional tickmarks (a cardinal-direction mnemonic)
- **Consonants** are grouped by morphological similarity to their Latin counterparts (mirror groups for B/D/P/Q, stroke-count groups for H/K/M/N)
- The system is deliberately mnemonic — designed for recall, not cryptographic security

Some glyph-level resemblances to Voynich characters exist: Fontana's plain circle (for I) matches EVA **o**; his double loop (for S) resembles EVA **l**; his parallel-stroke consonants echo EVA minims. But the overall system is too simple and one-dimensional (no vertical tier structure, no ascender/descender hierarchy) to account for the Voynich script.

Fontana's significance is not in direct authorship but in **proof of practice**: a Padua physician in the 1420s was designing invented scripts for illustrated technical manuscripts. And his degree promoter was the same Galeazzo di Santa Sofia who had spent 1394–1405 at Vienna.

### The Alchymey Teuczsch: Three Cipher Alphabets in Bavaria (1426)

The *Alchymey Teuczsch* (Heidelberg Cpg 597) was produced in 1426 by a circle of Bavarian alchemists near Passau. The manuscript contains **three invented cipher alphabets** with a cipher key on fol. 1r (later struck through). The authors explicitly stated:

> "We ourselves created this new alphabet so that one would not recognize it as an alphabet."

This is rare and specific: not just cipher, but glyphs deliberately designed to not look like writing — the same quality that characterizes the Voynich script.

Key features:
- The cipher alphabet includes single symbols for German digraphs **sch**, **ch**, and common words **vnd** ("and") and **ich** ("I") — encoding multi-character units as single glyphs, directly paralleling the Voynich's EVA digraphs (ch, sh)
- A **double secrecy layer**: beyond the cipher alphabets, the authors also used Decknamen (cover names) for substances — gold = "gelbe Erde" (yellow earth), silver = "weisse Erde" (white earth)
- **Selective encryption**: only alchemical formulas (transmutation, gold multiplication) were encrypted; medical recipes, astrological calendar, and liturgical content remained in clear text
- **Patronage connection**: the manuscript binding is a parchment diploma concerning the Jewish alchemist Salman Teublein's obligation to keep his art secret — knowledge transmission crossing the Jewish-Christian boundary under noble patronage

The cipher was deciphered by Gerhard Eis (published 1957) via German word-ending patterns — it was simple monoalphabetic substitution. But the design philosophy is the closest known parallel to the Voynich's approach: invented glyphs meant to look like something other than an alphabet, with multi-character units encoded as single symbols.

**No published visual comparison** of Cpg 597's cipher glyphs with Voynich EVA characters exists. The digital facsimile is freely available at Heidelberg. This is a concrete gap in the literature.

### Mellon MS 27: A Negative Comparand at Yale

Mellon MS 27, housed at the Beinecke Library alongside the Voynich (MS 408), is a c.1520–1586 alchemical miscellany using simple number-substitution cipher. At least five scribal hands contributed. The cipher was added c.1586 by Martin Roesel of Rosenthal to selected recipe passages — ordinary Arabic numerals standing for letters.

This manuscript matters not because it resembles the Voynich but because it shows what a **normal** alchemical cipher manuscript looks like: clear-text majority, selective encryption of sensitive recipes, trivially solvable substitution, identifiable underlying language. The Voynich violates every one of these norms — total encryption, invented writing system, unidentified language, and century-long resistance to all decryption methods. The contrast makes the Voynich's anomaly more precise.

Both manuscripts passed through H.P. Kraus's hands (Mellon MS 27 in 1949, the Voynich in 1961), but this appears coincidental — Kraus handled thousands of manuscripts.

### The Buch der heiligen Dreifaltigkeit (c.1410–1419)

This South German alchemical-theological text uses cipher grids and pastedown overlays to conceal content. The author presents alchemy as a spiritual practice with encoded symbols. It is the earliest known German alchemical text to use systematic cipher — predating the Alchymey Teuczsch by about a decade.

### The Broader Cipher Landscape

| System | Date | Type | Relation to Voynich |
|--------|------|------|-------------------|
| Papal nomenclators (Avignon) | 1379 | Letter substitution + code words | Diplomatic, not pharmaceutical |
| Buch der heiligen Dreifaltigkeit | c.1410–1419 | Cipher grids, overlays | South German alchemical context |
| Fontana (Padua) | c.1420 | Invented glyphs (circles + lines) | Closest design philosophy; one hop from Vienna |
| Alchymey Teuczsch (Bavaria) | 1426 | 3 invented alphabets + Decknamen | Same radiocarbon window; "not an alphabet" design |
| Alberti (Rome) | 1466 | First cryptography treatise | Theoretical, not practical |
| Simonetta (Milan) | 1474 | First cryptanalysis treatise | Diplomatic |
| Trithemius (Sponheim) | 1499 | Book-length cryptography as magic | Theoretical |

Every known cipher in this zone encrypts **natural language** — Latin, German, or both. Simple monoalphabetic substitution is universal. The Voynich's script produces text with statistical properties unlike any known substitution of any known language. Either the Voynich uses a fundamentally different encoding mechanism, or it encodes something other than natural language, or both.

## 6. The Supply Chain

The Voynich had multiple scribes, which implies institutional backing. Who had the infrastructure for coordinated manuscript production in 1404–1438?

### The Melk Reform Network

The strongest candidate is the **Melk Reform movement** — a network of Benedictine monasteries that began reforming in 1418, right in the Voynich radiocarbon window. The movement reinvigorated all aspects of monastic life across Austria and Southern Germany, with particular emphasis on scriptorial production.

**Melk Abbey** (Benedictine, Lower Austria): its scriptorium was "one of the most prolific in Europe," producing approximately 1,200 manuscripts, two-thirds from the reform period (post-1418). The abbey had direct connections to the University of Vienna and sat on the Danube with access to forest fuel for furnaces.

**Tegernsee Abbey** (Benedictine, Bavaria): under Abbot Kaspar Aindorfer (1426–1461), Tegernsee became "the centre of spiritual life in Bavaria," producing 1,680 manuscripts now held at the Bavarian State Library. The abbey maintained close personal relationships with both the University of Vienna and the reform monastery of Melk. Its *Liber illuministarum* (Cgm 821, c.1500) contains approximately 1,500 recipes across 231 folios — alchemy, metallurgy, medicine, distillation, pyrotechnics, pigments — compiled by eight scribes.

Critically, **approximately 460 of the 800 fifteenth-century Tegernsee manuscripts remain essentially uncatalogued** — the Bavarian State Library has only minimal nineteenth-century descriptions. Full cataloguing is described as "impossible for the foreseeable time."

**Klosterneuburg** (Augustinian, Lower Austria): Prior Mustinger's 500-florin Padua book purchase (1421) documents institutional investment in Mediterranean knowledge. Cod. 681 (1396) demonstrates that distillation treatises, zodiacal medicine, medical compilations, and the secrecy doctrine of the *Secretum Secretorum* all circulated together in a single codex.

**Heiligenkreuz** (Cistercian, Lower Austria): Cod. 331 contains a copy of Puff's distillation treatise alongside a color treatise, recipes, and a mistletoe treatise — confirming that pharmaceutical distillation knowledge physically circulated through the monastery network.

### Vienna's Apothecary Infrastructure

During the Voynich period (1404–1438), Vienna's apothecaries were **essentially unregulated**. The Medical Faculty tried to control them from the early fifteenth century, but apothecaries refused to comply and the town council sided with them. The dispute was not resolved until 1564.

A 1432 Vienna pharmacy inventory documents imports from Venice: saffron, ginger, cloves, cinnamon, nutmeg, camphor, alum, along with pharmaceutical chemicals including brimstone (sulphur), bluestone (copper sulphate), theriac, and mastic. This confirms the Venice–Brenner–Vienna trade route was active and supplying pharmaceutical raw materials during the Voynich period.

No formal apothecary guild existed — the earliest organized body dates to 1723. This was a free-market pharmaceutical environment.

### Glass and Apparatus

Distillation apparatus in this period used a two-tier glass system, documented at the Austrian site of Oberstockstall:
1. **Venetian cristallo** (sodium-rich, colorless) for distillation vessels requiring transparency
2. **Local Waldglas** (potassium-rich, greenish forest glass) for non-specialized containers

Brunschwig specifies "glassware from Bohemian or Venetian glassblowers" for distillation. The trade route ran: Venice/Murano → Fondaco dei Tedeschi → Brenner Pass → Innsbruck → (Inn Valley to Bavaria) or (Danube corridor to Vienna).

Alembics appeared in approximately 25% of Barcelona household inventories and 20% of Aix-en-Provence inventories — accessible to the upper-middle class, not just the wealthy. By the fifteenth century, glass was replacing pottery for distillation.

## 7. The Revelation: When Secrecy Broke

Brunschwig's 1512 *Large Book of Distillation* is the moment the secrecy regime broke. Its full title includes "geoffenbart" (revealed), and the word appears 90 times throughout the text. Brunschwig explicitly frames his project as bringing "*diese heimlicheit / vnd verborgene kunst an dz liecht*" — "this secrecy and hidden art to the light."

He names his sources: Arnoldus de Villa Nova, Marsilio Ficino, the unidentified Rupescissa Glossator. He acknowledges that what he is publishing was previously restricted knowledge. And he provides a motive — love of humanity, not commercial interest.

But Brunschwig's revelation is selective. He publishes procedures, materials, and apparatus designs. He does NOT publish whatever notation system the Vienna circle may have used for their internal records. He does NOT mention cipher, encoded writing, or unusual scripts anywhere in either his 1500 or 1512 books. His secrecy was about access, not encoding.

This means that if the Voynich encodes procedural knowledge from the pre-revelation period, its encoding system was separate from — and more sophisticated than — the secrecy practices Brunschwig acknowledged and broke. The Voynich would represent a layer of concealment that survived the "geoffenbart" moment.

## 8. What We Found in the Manuscripts

### Klosterneuburg Cod. 681 (1396)

This medical compendium, produced eight years before the Voynich vellum dating begins, combines:
- **Taddeo Alderotti's distillation treatise** (the founder of our distillation chain)
- **Ortolf von Baierland's Arzneibuch** (167 chapters organized head-to-toe, with systematic uroscopy, pulse diagnosis, and head-to-foot therapeutic protocols)
- **Secretum Secretorum** (German G3 version — the secrecy text, adapted for medical context)
- **De signis zodiaci** (all 12 zodiac signs with melothesia body-part governance, elemental properties, and a computation grid)
- **Dye and pigment recipes** (detailed instructions for blue, yellow, green, red, black, and white)

The manuscript demonstrates that a single codex could house medical knowledge, astrological timing, distillation procedures, secrecy doctrine, AND artisanal craft knowledge — all in one binding, at one institution, within the Voynich's zone.

The zodiac section (fols. 159v–164v) treats all 12 signs with melothesia body-part governance, house assignments, and operational timing ("from mid-March to mid-April"). Each sign governs approximately 30 days — potentially explaining the Voynich AZC pages' 30 nymphs per zodiac emblem. But the Klosterneuburg zodiac is purely textual (no circular diagrams, no zodiac man illustration), while the Voynich uses elaborate visual wheels. They represent opposite ends of the same tradition.

### Brunschwig's 1512 Large Book

Systematic reading of all 92,557 lines confirmed:
- "Geoffenbart" appears 90 times (394 all forms of "offenbar") — programmatic, not casual
- The Rupescissa Glossator is the primary quintessence authority — unidentified, active at Padua 1463
- Puff is NOT cited by name
- Zero references to cipher, coded writing, or unusual notation
- Fire degrees follow Avicenna's four-level system
- Arnoldus and Ficino named as Aurum potabile secret-keepers

### Vienna Medical Faculty Acta (1436–1501)

- Sworn institutional secrecy confirmed in the decanal oath
- Puff's personal custody of the registrum receptarum documented
- Clandestine dissection (1452) handled "occulte"
- Stainpeis testimony (~1520) records Puff's surviving unpublished experimenta

### Texts That Produced No Leads

- **Tichtel diary (1477–1495):** Zero cipher/alchemy/Puff content
- **Ebendorfer chronicle:** Court occultism documented but no encoding practices
- **Piccolomini correspondence (1444):** Confirms the Vienna–Padua pipeline but no cipher specifics
- **Celtis–Trithemius correspondence:** 12-year gap covering the *Steganographia* period; silence is suggestive but not actionable

## 9. The Gap

Everything in this essay converges on a specific gap:

**We can document the institutional secrecy. We can document the distillation knowledge. We can document the cipher practices. We can document the manuscript production infrastructure. We can document the personnel connections. But we cannot find the moment where someone decided to encode procedural knowledge — not natural language, but operational procedures — in an invented writing system.**

Every known cipher in this zone encrypts natural language (Latin or German) using simple monoalphabetic substitution. The Voynich's script produces text with different statistical properties. Either:

1. The Voynich uses a more sophisticated cipher than anything else documented in the zone (possible but unconfirmed)
2. The Voynich encodes something other than natural language — procedures, parameters, operational states — which would explain why substitution-based decryption fails (consistent with our structural analysis)
3. Both: a novel encoding applied to procedural content

The second option aligns with our project's 1,220 validated structural constraints, which describe the Voynich's Currier B text as closed-loop control programs — kernel-centric, state-maintaining, convergent. This is not what encrypted prose looks like. It is what encoded operational procedures look like.

The historical network shows that the knowledge, the secrecy motivation, the institutional capacity, and the cipher technology all existed in the right place at the right time. What is missing is the bridge: the document, person, or practice that connects "we encode natural language with invented glyphs" (Fontana, Alchymey Teuczsch) to "we encode operational procedures in a systematic grammar" (the Voynich).

## 10. The Provenance Chain: Where the Manuscript Went

Everything in sections 2–9 describes the world that *could have* produced the Voynich manuscript. This section traces where the manuscript *actually went* — as far as the documentary record allows.

### The First Confirmed Owner: An Imperial Distiller

The earliest name physically inscribed on the Voynich manuscript — visible under ultraviolet light on folio 1r — is **Jacobus de Tepenecz**. This is Jacobus Horcicky de Tepenec (c.1575–1622), who was appointed **Imperial Chemist** by Rudolf II in 1607. His Czech surname "Horcicky" derives from "horcice" (mustard); his Latinized name "Sinapius" is the Latin equivalent.

Horcicky's career was built on distillation. He began as a kitchen helper at the Jesuit school in Cesky Krumlov, trained in pharmacy, and while at the Clementinum college in Prague created **Aqua Sinapis** — a distilled mustard water that made him enormously wealthy. He ran an herb laboratory at the Smichov botanical garden in Prague. He lent Rudolf II such large sums that he received the estate of Melnik in return. When he died in 1622, he left 50,000 gold coins and the Melnik estate to the Jesuits.

This fact was established through physical evidence (UV-visible signature on the manuscript) and archival records — entirely independent of any analysis of the manuscript's textual content. The convergence between Horcicky's profession and our structural analysis (which independently concluded the Voynich encodes process-control procedures consistent with distillation) was not predicted or expected.

### The Purchase: 600 Ducats

Jan Marek Marci's 1665 letter to Athanasius Kircher reports — via the cryptographer Raphael Mnishovsky — that Rudolf II paid **600 ducats** for the manuscript. In 2022, Stefan Guzy (University of the Arts Bremen) searched the complete imperial account books. Out of nearly 7,000 journal entries including 126 book transactions, only **one** matched 600 gold coins: a sale by **Karl Widemann** on March 1, 1599, for "a small barrel with a couple of remarkable/rare books" — 500 Taler, equivalent to 600 Rheinische Gulden.

Widemann was a physician-turned-manuscript-dealer who had studied at the **University of Padua**, worked in Prague as secretary to the English alchemist **Edward Kelley** (1587-1588), and lived in the former Augsburg house of the botanist **Leonhard Rauwolf** (d. 1596). The theory is circumstantial — the archival records never name the Voynich specifically — but the 600-ducat match is unique in Rudolf's financial records.

### The Chain After Rudolf

Rudolf gave the manuscript to Horcicky, probably as partial payment for debts. After Horcicky's death (1622), the manuscript somehow reached **Georg Baresch**, a Prague alchemist and court reporter who had also studied at the Clementinum. By 1637, Baresch had possessed it for "many years." He described it as a "Sphinx" and identified its content as medical, herbal, and chemical.

Baresch bequeathed his library to **Jan Marek Marci**, Chief Physician of Bohemia and Rector of Charles University, who sent the manuscript to Athanasius Kircher in Rome (1665). It remained in Jesuit custody at the Collegio Romano for approximately 200 years. In 1912, it was sold to the antiquarian Wilfrid Voynich. It passed to his widow (1930), then to his secretary Anne Nill (1960), then to dealer H.P. Kraus (1961), who donated it to Yale (1969).

### The Missing Jesuit Mark

A critical anomaly: three other books bearing Horcicky's ownership mark survive today, and all three bear a Jesuit library ownership note — entered when Horcicky's possessions passed to the Jesuits at his death. The Voynich has no such note. Either the manuscript left Horcicky's hands before death, or it was separated from his estate before Jesuit cataloguing. The institutional overlap between Horcicky and Baresch (both connected to the Clementinum in Prague, same decade) suggests a more direct transfer.

### The Swedish Sack

In 1648, Swedish troops looted Prague Castle, taking hundreds of paintings, instruments, and curiosities. The Voynich survived because it was in private hands (Baresch), not in institutional collections. If it had remained in Rudolf's Kunstkammer, it would likely have been lost.

### 2024 Multispectral Imaging

In September 2024, reprocessed multispectral images from the 2014 Lazarus Project revealed new details:

- **Marci's decipherment attempt:** Three parallel columns on fol. 1r — Roman alphabet, Voynichese, offset Roman alphabet — an early substitution cipher attempt that produces nonsense. Paleographically attributed to Marci (c. 1640). Confirms the manuscript is not a simple substitution cipher.
- **Germanic marginal inscriptions** on fols. 17r and 116v, roughly contemporary with the manuscript, possibly incantations. Previously illegible.
- **Production sequence confirmed:** Images were drawn and colored before the text was written (fol. 93r).
- **Hidden drawings** beneath stains on fol. 102v1.

### The Two Gaps

Two critical gaps remain in the provenance chain:

1. **The 160-year lacuna (c. 1440 – c. 1590s):** No record of the manuscript between its creation and its probable appearance in the Widemann-Rudolf transaction. If produced in the Vienna-Padua zone, how did it reach Augsburg or Prague? Possible channels: monastery network dispersal, university manuscript circulation, Blotius's documented transfers of alchemical books from Vienna to Prague at Rudolf's orders.

2. **Horcicky to Baresch (1622–1637):** Horcicky's will left his possessions to the Jesuits, but the Voynich lacks the Jesuit library mark. Both men were connected to the Clementinum. The transfer mechanism is unconfirmed.

## 12. What This Does Not Claim

1. **No authorship attribution.** No person in this network is claimed as the Voynich's author.
2. **No complete provenance chain.** The ownership history has two documented gaps (160 years and 15 years).
3. **No decipherment.** Historical parallels and provenance do not decode the text.
4. **No geographic certainty.** The network maps the zone of possibility, not the point of origin.
5. **No causal claims.** Documented connections (Galeazzo bridging Fontana and Vienna, Puff's secretive custody, Brunschwig's revelation, Horcicky's distillation career) are presented as context, not as proof of Voynich involvement.

This is infrastructure for evaluation. Any future provenance or authorship claim can be tested against this network: does the claimed author have documented connections to the persons, institutions, and knowledge traditions mapped here? If not, the claim has a problem. If so, the claim has a foundation.
