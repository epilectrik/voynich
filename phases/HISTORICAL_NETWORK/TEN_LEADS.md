# Phase 491: Ten Leads for a Voynich Connection

**Compiled:** 2026-02-28
**Source:** Primary source reading of 10 digitized texts + network analysis of 41 persons, 67 edges, 16 cipher parallels

These are ranked by specificity and actionability — each lead has a concrete thing to check.

## Lead 1: The Rupescissa Glossator (Padua, 1463)

**What we found:** Brunschwig's 1512 Large Book cites "der glosierer uber Johannes Rupescissus" as his primary quintessence authority — not Rupescissa directly. This unidentified commentator publicly defended quintessence theory at the University of Padua in 1463, "in einer offenbarlichen versamlung der artzet" (in a public assembly of physicians).

**Why it matters:** This is an unidentified person at the exact center of the Rupescissa → practical distillation chain, active at Padua during the peak of the Vienna-Padua pipeline, one year before Regiomontanus lectured there (1464). If the Voynich encodes distillation procedures, this person operated in the exact milieu where such a system could originate.

**What to check:**
- Padua University records for 1463 — who participated in medical disputations?
- Does the gloss/commentary on Rupescissa survive as an independent text?
- Did Regiomontanus (Padua 1464) encounter this glossator?
- Is this person identifiable from other Brunschwig citations or from manuscript attributions?

## Lead 2: Puff's Lost Registrum Receptarum

**What we found:** The Vienna Medical Faculty Acta document that Michael Puff von Schrick personally held the *registrum receptarum* (pharmaceutical recipe register) during his 11 deanships (1435-1473). Stainpeis testified (~1520) that Puff left "multa egregia experimenta" whose fame "has not been extinguished."

**Why it matters:** This is a pharmaceutical procedure register from the exact institution, time period, and secrecy context as the Voynich. It was held under sworn institutional secrecy (decanal oath). If it survives, it could show what notation was used for procedural knowledge at the Vienna Medical Faculty.

**What to check:**
- ÖNB (Austrian National Library) manuscript catalog for anything attributed to Puff beyond the printed Büchlein
- Vienna University Archive for faculty documents from 1435-1500
- Handschriftencensus.de entries for Puff/Schrick
- Did Kargl (1499) or Planckh (1553) inherit it? Planckh's expanded edition cites additional sources.

## Lead 3: South German Cursive Hand — Glyph Borrowing

**What we found:** Pal. lat. 1396 (Heidelberg, ~1500), an astrological-astronomical miscellany, uses a South German cursive hand where specific characters visually match Voynich glyphs: 9→y, 8→l, 4→q, plus loop and m-shapes. At a glance the page looks like Voynichese; on close inspection it's standard German/Latin.

**Why it matters:** If the Voynich author's hand was trained in a South German cursive tradition, the borrowed vs. invented glyph split could identify: (a) where the author was trained, and (b) what the natural writing system couldn't express — potentially corresponding to the functional elements of the Voynich grammar (kernel operators, category markers, morphological slots).

**What to check:**
- Systematic glyph-by-glyph comparison: which Voynich characters have exact matches in South German cursive? Which have no parallel?
- Do the "invented" Voynich glyphs cluster in specific morphological positions (PREFIX, MIDDLE, SUFFIX)?
- Compare with other dated South German manuscripts from 1400-1450 to narrow the scribal tradition
- Lisa Fagin Davis or other Voynich paleographers may have already done partial comparisons

## Lead 4: Alchymey Teuczsch Cipher Glyphs (Cpg 597, fol. 1r)

**What we found:** The Alchymey Teuczsch (1426, eastern Bavaria) has three invented cipher alphabets with a cipher key on fol. 1r (later struck through). The authors explicitly said "We ourselves created this new alphabet so that one would not recognize it as an alphabet." Full digital facsimile freely available at Heidelberg.

**Why it matters:** These are invented glyphs from the exact Voynich radiocarbon window (1404-1438), in the Voynich's geographic zone, designed to look like they're not an alphabet. A visual comparison with Voynich glyphs has never been published.

**What to check:**
- Download fol. 1r from https://digi.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/diglit/cpg597
- Systematic visual comparison of all 3 cipher alphabets with EVA characters
- Do any Alchymey Teuczsch glyphs match Voynich characters?
- If some match and some don't, does the split tell us anything about shared vs. independent invention?

## Lead 5: Brunschwig's Uncited Puff Incorporation

**What we found:** Brunschwig does NOT cite Puff by name in EITHER the 1500 Small Book or the 1512 Large Book. Modern scholars (identified through textual comparison) showed that Brunschwig incorporated Puff's Büchlein material word-for-word into the Small Book without attribution.

**Why it matters:** This reveals a hidden transmission chain. Puff's *procedural knowledge* was absorbed into the published tradition without acknowledgment, meaning the published works conceal their actual sources. If the Voynich was another product of Puff's circle, it could have entered circulation equally invisibly.

**What to check:**
- Which specific Puff chapters does Brunschwig reproduce? (Textual comparison of our Puff 1501 text vs. Brunschwig 1500 text)
- Are there passages in Brunschwig that don't match Puff or any other identified source — i.e., unattributed material from the Vienna tradition?
- Did the Rupescissa Glossator also transmit through Puff? (Puff → Brunschwig pathway for Rupescissa material)

## Lead 6: Fontana's Cipher System (BSB Cod.icon. 242)

**What we found:** Giovanni Fontana (Padua physician, medical degree 1421) used entirely invented signs — no letters or numbers — in his Bellicorum instrumentorum liber (c.1420). The cipher uses 23 symbols constructed from circles and straight lines as a mnemonic system. Fully deciphered — it's simple monoalphabetic substitution of Latin.

**Why it matters:** This is the only known Padua physician using invented glyphs during the Voynich's radiocarbon window. His symbols were designed to be systematic (vowels = circles with tickmarks, consonants = stroke-count systems). This is closer to the Voynich's script design than any standard letter substitution.

**What to check:**
- Download cipher pages from BSB digital (https://www.digitale-sammlungen.de/en/details/bsb00013084)
- Compare Fontana's 23 symbols with Voynich EVA characters
- Fontana's symbol construction rules (circles + lines) — do any Voynich glyphs follow the same construction logic?
- Voynich Portal has detailed analysis: https://voynichportal.com/2020/07/20/fontanas-cipher-design/

## Lead 7: The Vienna Secrecy Regime — Institutional Documentation

**What we found:** Three layers of documented secrecy at the Vienna Medical Faculty:
1. Decanal oath: "*singula facultatis secreta nullatenus revelare velit*" (reveal none of the faculty's secrets)
2. Puff held the registrum receptarum personally for 38 years
3. Clandestine dissection (1452) handled "*occulte*"

**Why it matters:** This documents sworn, institutional secrecy at the exact institution connected to the Voynich's probable milieu. It's not individual quirks — it's policy. The question is whether this institutional secrecy extended to encoding practices.

**What to check:**
- Do the Vienna Acta mention any written codes, special notations, or abbreviation systems used by the faculty?
- Were there other secret registers or documents beyond the registrum receptarum?
- Did other Central European medical faculties have similar sworn secrecy? (Comparanda from Padua, Prague, Erfurt)
- The Acta extend to 1501 — do later entries mention Puff's materials after his death?

## Lead 8: Regiomontanus at Padua (1464) → Nuremberg Secrecy (1473)

**What we found:** Regiomontanus lectured at Padua in 1464 — one year after the Rupescissa Glossator's public defense there. By 1473, Schedel's correspondence documents him operating in Nuremberg under extreme secrecy: locked house, secret printing, travel to Italy for books.

**Why it matters:** Regiomontanus is one of the few documented persons who was at Padua during the Glossator's activity AND later operated under extreme secrecy. He was an astronomer/mathematician, not a medical man, but the Vienna-Padua pipeline carried all disciplines.

**What to check:**
- Regiomontanus's correspondence (some published) — any mention of medical/alchemical manuscripts?
- His Padua lectures (1464) — who attended? Were medical faculty present?
- What was he printing secretly in Nuremberg? Only astronomical tables, or other content?
- His Italian book-buying trips — any alchemical or medical purchases documented?

## Lead 9: Mellon MS 27 — Yale's Other Cipher Manuscript

**What we found:** Mellon MS 27, at the Beinecke Library (same institution as the Voynich), is a 15th-16th century German-speaking manuscript using substitution cipher for selected alchemical recipes. 5+ scribal hands, cipher passages notably on f.23r.

**Why it matters:** Another cipher manuscript in the same building, from the same cultural zone and period. If the cipher system or any glyphs match the Voynich, that's a direct connection. Even if they don't match, comparing the two manuscripts' approaches to selective encryption could be informative.

**What to check:**
- Beinecke digital images of Mellon MS 27 (should be freely available)
- Agnieszka Rec's Societas Magica 2014 analysis — does it include glyph images?
- Compare cipher alphabet with Voynich EVA characters
- What is encrypted vs. clear? Does the selection logic match the Voynich's "everything encrypted" pattern or the Alchymey Teuczsch's "selective encryption" pattern?

## Lead 10: The Padua 1463 Disputation — University Records

**What we found:** Brunschwig explicitly cites a public academic disputation at the University of Padua in 1463 where the Rupescissa Glossator defended quintessence theory before an assembly of physicians.

**Why it matters:** Public academic disputations were formal, recorded events. If the Padua university archives preserve records from 1463, we might identify the glossator by name — and potentially discover their connections to Vienna, the German-speaking world, and the broader network.

**What to check:**
- Padua University historical records (Atti della Nazione Germanica Artista, published by Favaro and others)
- The Acta Nationis Germanicae Artistarum (records of German students at Padua) — published editions exist
- 1463 medical faculty records — who was defending, who was examining?
- Cross-reference any names found with Vienna Medical Faculty Acta from the same period
